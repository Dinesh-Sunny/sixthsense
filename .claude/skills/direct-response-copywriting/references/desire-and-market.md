# Desire, Research & Market (from a performance-ads masterclass)

Works for any channel: ads, pages, emails, VSLs. Almost nothing here is new since Schwartz (1966); it's the classics turned into steps. Use frameworks to become competent, then break them once you've earned it.

## Start from desire, not product

- Copy can't create desire. It channels hopes, fears and desires that already exist onto your product (Schwartz). Briefs that start with the product and work backwards to a reason to care produce flat copy.
- **Evidence test:** if you can't find the desire somewhere *outside* your own marketing (a review, a support ticket, a forum thread, a call), you don't have a concept. You have a product description with a hook stuck on.
- **Starving crowd** (Halbert): the best advantage is a hungry audience. Online, the message finds that crowd. Every word either signals "this is for you" or makes them feel it's slightly off.
- A specific detail in the opening can target, prove and cast all at once. "Discovered by a one-legged golfer" isn't aimed at one-legged golfers; it proves the secret isn't athleticism.
- Being unique is worthless if it doesn't connect to a desire people have had for a long time.

## Kill table-stakes claims

Test each differentiator with three questions:
1. Is it a **proposition**, i.e. "buy this, get this"? ("Great service" is a claim, not a proposition.)
2. Is it **unavailable to competitors**?
3. Is it **strong enough to move a stranger**?

Usually fails: family-owned since 19xx, made in [country], vegan/cruelty-free (when everyone is), free shipping. Usually passes: published third-party lab results (costly, so rare).
- Don't answer objections nobody has. Often the best edit is to **delete** the line, not improve it.
- Stacking generic USPs ("great fragrance *and* gentle") is a weak fallback, not a strategy.
- Copying a competitor's longest-running ad doesn't copy their brand trust, proof, or audience belief. It worked *for them*.

## Research sources, ranked by yield

| Source | Yield / effort | What only this source gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Customer calls | high / high | The **final objection** before buying ("I nearly didn't…") |
| Support tickets & returns | high / low | **Failure modes**, in escalating language. Never promise what fails. |
| Reviews | high / lowest | Customers' **exact words**. 2–4★ reviews = objections. |
| Post-purchase survey | medium / low | What they were thinking **at the moment of purchase** (not usage) |
| Forums, Reddit, groups | medium / medium | Language of **people who haven't bought**, and where competitors fail |

Work top-down if you have customers, bottom-up if you're pre-launch. Clean up transcripts with an LLM before searching them. Use LLMs to **retrieve quotes with links, never to invent** customer insight: invented insight comes out generic.

Use objections from reviews to improve the product too. Scripting around a flaw you haven't fixed is deceptive.

## Five things to dig out (each feeds one part of the piece)

| Find | Question | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| **Accommodation** | What did they change in their life to work around the problem, without admitting it? | Problem |
| **Failed solution** | What did they try first, what did it cost, why did they quit? | Objection handling |
| **Nearly didn't buy** | What almost stopped them? | Risk reversal / offer |
| **The word** | What noun do *they* use for the problem? | Hook |
| **The moment** | What happened the day they started looking? | Urgency |

Customer words beat marketer words: "looked four months pregnant by 4 p.m." beats "bloating"; "can't get off the floor without help" beats "joint support"; "I can see my scalp" beats "hair thinning." Marketer words make the reader translate.

## Concepts & volume

Concept = **persona × angle × offer**. A few personas times a few angles gives a dozen concepts, and each can carry many variants: different scripts, swapped hooks, different formats (talking head, animation, founder story). Do research in batches, not per ad.

## Awareness × sophistication

**Awareness** (Schwartz): unaware → problem-aware → solution-aware → product-aware → most aware. Most people sit at the top. Colder levels cost more and take far more skill; good unaware copy is rare.

| Level | Example opening (hair-loss supplement) |
|---|---|
| Unaware | "The photos you hate of yourself are usually taken from behind, and there's a reason." |
| Problem-aware | "If you've started parting it on the side, this is for you." |
| Solution-aware | "Most supplements use the key ingredient at a fifth of the studied dose." |
| Product/most aware | "The 90-day pack is back. Last run at this price." |

**Sophistication** (how tired the market is of claims):
1. First to market: a plain claim works.
2. Claims inflate ("strongest on the market").
3. Unique mechanism ("it's not the hair, it's the follicle").
4. Mechanisms inflate (bigger dose, better delivery).
5. Exhausted: sell identity and transformation ("the ones who keep it started before they could see it").

- Copy that works at a later stage usually beats the plain claim even in an early market.
- Label each piece with its awareness and sophistication level. **Everything in one campaign must match**: visual, voiceover, headline, body text, landing page. Mixed levels confuse the reader and weaken ad targeting.
- Mix levels across *different* pieces to reach more of the market ("message-to-market match," Kennedy).

## Offer outranks copy

Order of importance: **offer > market > mechanism > proof > everything else.** A bad product kills repeat purchase; acquisition costs rise and marketing alone can't fix that.

Offer parts the copy depends on:
- **Thing:** what I get, in my words.
- **Terms:** what it costs me.
- **Reversal:** what happens if it doesn't work.
- **Reason for now.**
- **Effort:** what I must do, and how long it takes.

A copywriter rarely controls price but can **reframe what already exists**: free returns become a confident guarantee, a hidden 3-pack becomes "the 90-day pack," real urgency already in the product gets named. An offer is a reframe of value, not just a discount.

**Pricing frames:**
- Anchor against the alternative ("one specialist visit costs $200 and they'll tell you to take this").
- Reduce to the ridiculous (cents per day versus a coffee).
- Price the problem, not the product ("you've already spent more on two things that didn't work").
- Make low effort the selling point ("one gummy with breakfast").

**Guarantees that sell:**
- Specific: "take it 90 days; if your part hasn't changed, send back the empty tubs and we refund all three."
- Slightly uncomfortable for the seller. A guarantee that costs you nothing proves nothing.
- Matched to the mechanism's timeline.
- Repeats the core message.
