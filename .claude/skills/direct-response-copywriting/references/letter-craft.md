# Letter Craft

## Drafting order (Carlton)

1. **Bullets** — dump every feature/benefit; this is the product's essence.
2. **Opening / setup** — why I'm writing to you; grabber tie-in if any.
3. **Close** — offer, guarantee, call to action, P.S.
4. **Headline** — last, once you know the 1–4 themes. It often writes itself.

Expect many drafts. One Halbert/Carlton letter took 17 drafts (16th was the worst; 17th mailed for 18 months). Halbert's first winning letter took 18 months of revision and later mailed hundreds of millions of copies.

## Headlines

- Carry ~80% of the load; scan order is headline → photo → caption → subheads → body.
- Proven shapes:
  - Story/discovery: "Grand Rapids agent discovers simple secret of earning $X working only 40 hours a week"
  - Desperation → result: "The amazing money-making secret of a desperate man who almost lost his [business]"
  - "New book reveals 37 little-known ways to [result]"
  - "How a $7 investment can boost your [X] by as much as $60,000"
  - "They all laughed when… but…" (classic swipe structure)
  - News/curiosity: "[City] invaded by bugs that live in bedroom carpets" (never names the business)
  - Insider exposé: "Former employee of [trade magazine] finally reveals the dirty little secret used by the most profitable [dealers]"
- Use a lead-in (pre-head) and a sub-head: "Now at last you can use it too" / "The amazing thing is how fast…"
- Upper and lower case, not all caps; not justified block.
- Bad sign: you can't remember the headline after reading it; it has no benefit; it's about the seller.

## Bullets

- Each bullet is a mini sales pitch: feature + explicit benefit, expanded so it plants a picture.
- Write more than you think; never pre-guess which will sell — often one obscure bullet closes.
- Mix blind bullets (curiosity: "the four magic questions top producers use…") with specific-number bullets ("how to get appointments with at least 70% of the people you talk to").
- Same benefit (e.g. "saves time") can appear in several bullets from different features/angles.
- Entire ads can be: one-paragraph intro ("X has just published a new book that shows Y how to Z. Here are some of the secrets it reveals:") → bullets → "It's easy to order" close. Ultra-fast template when short on time.
- The rest of the letter mostly exists to get bullets read and believed.

## Story

- First-person transformation: pain (specific, humiliating, financial) → discovery (by accident, from an unlikely source) → first result with exact numbers → repeatable results → reluctant decision to share (with a reason) → offer.
- Third-person variant: narrator meets someone (affluent stranger, old-timer) who reveals a secret; reader must call to learn it. Builds curiosity without revealing mechanism.
- Customer-story variant for the next ad in the series: a buyer tells how they almost didn't respond, then did ("even the phone call was free").
- When one story wears out, write another — a campaign can run 20+ different stories to the same market.

## Readability & flow

- Greased slide: every element's job is to get the next one read.
- Bucket brigades / transitions at paragraph ends.
- Split sentences across page/screen breaks; ellipsis leading into a subhead that continues the sentence ("In fact… / the only way you can get this now…").
- Subheads are continuation + selling points, not section titles.
- Short paragraphs, indented; centered subheads; eye relief. Color is a minor touch — last thing to worry about.
- Write like talking to one friend. Long copy is fine if it's interesting; it gives room to repeat points in new ways.
- Skimmers are unpredictable — don't over-engineer for them; just make subheads tell the story.

## Common faults (from live critiques)

- Pompous opening about the writer ("I'm kind of a marketing guru…").
- Clever asides and clichés ("faster than a politician can lie") — draw attention to the writing.
- Telling the method too early in lead-gen copy.
- "Here's what you'll learn" instead of "here's what happened / what you'll get."
- Questions where statements of outcome are stronger ("Do you detest price competition?" → "Eliminate price competition forever").
- Wall-of-text paragraphs, justified headlines, too much copy crammed around the CTA.
- Referencing something (e.g. "the program") before introducing it.
