# Script Anatomy: 9 Beats (from a performance-ads masterclass)

Applies to video scripts, sales pages, emails, statics and advertorials. On a static ad, the beats that don't fit move to the landing page.

## The beats, in order

1. **Hook:** buys attention and pre-qualifies the right people. The hook is where most of the *audience* is; it persuades almost no one. The other ~95% of the piece does the selling.
2. **Problem:** agitate the consequence they've already accommodated.
3. **Promise:** what they get, in their terms, in one sentence.
4. **Mechanism:** why the promise is believable.
5. **Objection:** answer, in advance, what they tried before.
6. **Proof:** back up the claim.
7. **Offer:** present the product and terms.
8. **Risk reversal:** what happens if it doesn't work.
9. **Call to action.**

## Hook

- Write hooks *with* line 2. Ten hooks pasted onto the same body break the flow and readers drop off.
- Native (looks like content: notes app, screenshot, talking to camera) usually outperforms obvious-ad polish. Polished brand shots still help with perceived value.
- Marketing language, more than production quality, is what makes something read as an ad. Strip it from the opening.
- Every hook surface must say the same thing: first frame, voiceover, text overlay, primary text, headline, landing page. Luxury visual plus cheap voiceover plus sale text plus science headline = incoherent.
- Weak: "Introducing the new summer collection." Strong: "I ordered this dress in three sizes because I had no idea which one I was" (then resolve the sizing worry later).

## Problem

- **Agitate the consequence, never the symptom.** The symptom (hair in the drain) is something they already know and anyone can say. The accommodation (stopped parting it in the middle) is something they've never heard said out loud.
- Three ways in: the **accommodation**, the **moment** ("a wedding photo from behind"), a **failed solution** (which also makes the piece solution-aware).
- About 10–15 seconds in short formats. **Escalate, don't list:** if you can reorder the lines without changing the argument, it's a list of symptoms, and that reads as complaining.
- Agitate **one person**, at the **category** level (don't name your product yet), and **don't resolve** it in the problem section.
- Brands that "don't do negative" can still frame the accommodation: it's an observation, not pity. Pity comes from piling up symptoms and treating the reader as a sufferer.
- Test switching person: "You've stopped…" versus "I stopped…".

## Promise → mechanism

- Failure modes: no promise; five promises; a feature presented as a promise ("six clinically studied ingredients"); a promise bigger than the proof.
- **A smaller promise you can prove beats a bigger one you can't** (Gary Bencivenga).
- Chain: **feature → which means [promise] → which is why [mechanism].**
- **Promise before mechanism.** Jargon first loses people before the payoff ("lowers ghrelin…" ✗ → "you stop thinking about food at 11 a.m., because…" ✓).
- Every promise needs a mechanism. If a feature has none, find one, swap the feature, or fall back to promise + proof.
- **A contradiction isn't a mechanism.** "Energy without the crash" needs a *why* (L-theanine cancels the jitters). Without one, use it as a hook or cut it.
- Every product has a mechanism: someone made a decision that had a consequence (fabric, construction, process, dose, a supplier test).
- Don't name the product until the mechanism has landed; naming it first drops you into the commodity bucket.
- Bridge into the product gently: "the fix sits upstream of the shampoo aisle… three things slow it down… you can buy them separately, or take one capsule." Natural content flows thought → small complaint → story → product.

## Specificity

- Ladder: numbers → a named third party → a physical detail → a specific time → proper nouns. Adjectives are the writer's opinion; specifics are evidence.
  - "Fast absorbing" → "dry in 40 seconds; we timed it."
  - "Luxury craftsmanship" → "one person stitches each pair start to finish. It takes her four hours."
- **Hopkins' Schlitz story:** tell the process everyone does but nobody mentions. Ask what your production does that seems boring, what you rejected during development, and what constraint the product forced on you.
- **Honest urgency**: real numbers and dates ("next batch ships the 14th; October's already full"), never "limited time only."
- You can be specific without overclaiming. Specify the process, the dose and the duration rather than promising outcomes you can't back.

## Objections (handle them in this order)

| Objection | Fix |
|---|---|
| This isn't for me | Language specific to this persona |
| It won't work | Mechanism + proof |
| It works, but not for me | Proof from someone like *them* |
| Not worth it | Offer, value, cost of doing nothing |
| What if I'm wrong? | Guarantee / risk reversal |
| I'll do it later | Real urgency |

Answer objections before they come up, inside the customer's own experience: "You'll react to this in week one. Everyone does. That's why the first tube is low-dose." This beats "gentle enough for sensitive skin."

## Proof ladder (strongest first)

1. **Demonstration:** it working on camera, before/after.
2. **Third-party verification:** clinical results, lab tests, independent press.
3. **Volume of specific social proof:** "4,812 reviews; the word that comes up most is *finally*."
4. **Named individual testimony:** in B2B, one recognizable client beats thousands of anonymous reviews.
5. **Mechanism explanation:** logic as proof.
6. **Founder assertion:** only if the founder has real standing.

- **Proof must be at least as big as the claim.** A modest claim with strong proof beats a big claim with weak proof.
- With no proof yet: lean on demonstration, and gather detailed reviews from early users. Disclose any free product or incentive where the law requires.

## Call to action

Name the action, say what happens next, show how little effort it takes, and give a reason to act now.
- "Learn more" ✗ → "Take the 60-second quiz. You'll get your size and two cuts that suit your shape" ✓ (plus a real reason for now).

## Emotion & identity

- **"So what?" test** on every line. The final answer must be a human reason, not a mechanism. Believing isn't the same as wanting; a script can be correct line by line and move no one.
- Join the conversation already happening in their head (Collier): meet them where their attention already is, in a voice that belongs there, *then* introduce your subject.
- Connect each mechanism to an identity. DHT-blocking → "someone who acts before it shows"; 40-second absorption → "out the door at 6:30"; third-party tests → "someone who checks."
- Identity without a mechanism is lifestyle fluff. A mechanism without identity is a checklist: competent, forgettable.
- Strong pattern: **end on an identity line** ("The women who keep it are the ones who started before they could see it"). It doubles as a qualifier.

## Story as the delivery vehicle

- A story isn't a beat. It carries all nine. In a story the reader stops judging you and wants to know what happened.
- Four common shapes:
  - **Failed-solution journey:** objections.
  - **Discovery:** mechanism.
  - **Transformation with a witness:** proof ("my sister noticed first").
  - **Confession:** problem.
- What breaks it: a clumsy jump from story into pitch; a narrator who's obviously acting; **no stakes** ("I'd been looking for something" ✗ → "I stopped going to the hairdresser because I thought she'd say something" ✓).
- Default to first person, past tense. Don't resolve in the hook.
- **Stories must be true.** Source them from customer research. A story that didn't happen is fiction and must read as fiction.
- Third-person, brand, and historical "rediscovered remedy" stories work in impersonal categories too.

## Writing for the ear (and plain prose)

- One idea per sentence. Verb early. Plain words.
  - "Follicular miniaturization occurs when…" ✗
  - "There's a hormone that binds to the follicle. Every cycle the hair comes back finer. Then one cycle it doesn't come back." ✓
- Vary sentence length (e.g. long, long, short). All-short staccato and "it's not X, it's Y" contrast are tells of AI-written copy.
- Match reading level to the audience (around grade 5–7 for mass market; higher for experts). Always use contractions. End on an important word.
- **Read it aloud, standing, at speaking pace.** Wherever you trip, run out of breath, or cringe, rewrite.
- Same angle in a different register makes a new variant: casual second person, flat documentary third person, diary-style first person.

## Format & length

- Choose the format *after* the script, based on the **load-bearing beat**: an invisible edge → mechanism (animation shows the unshowable); a visible edge → proof (demo); a better deal → offer.
- Score formats on six properties: credible human face, can show what a camera can't, controls the order things are seen, has sound, holds attention, reads as a person. Many "production failures" are really the script asking a format to carry something it can't.
- Longer pieces scale further because they can start colder. Grow the problem, mechanism, objections and proof, not the hook or offer. Loop the beats (e.g. a day 7 / 14 / 30 / 90 timeline) and include multiple calls to action in very long pieces.
- **Write for each length.** Cutting a long script down rarely works; rewrite the short version so its transitions still work.

## AI in the workflow

- Models drift toward the average. Feed them your research corpus and the classic copywriters' material, and tell them to **retrieve, not invent** (including mechanisms).
- Good uses: transposing a winner into a new person, tense or format; generating variants; auditing a draft against these beats ("missing a promise → give me five options"); a first draft to get past a blank page.
- The human job is **selection**. The research and thinking was always the hard 80%.
