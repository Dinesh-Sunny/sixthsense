# Sharpness Tests (Harry Dry)

Line-level tests for making copy memorable and believable. Apply to every headline and every sentence that carries a claim.

## The three tests

Ask of each sentence:

1. **Can I visualize it?** If you can't see it, the reader won't remember it.
2. **Can I falsify it?** Could it be proved true or false? Checkable claims make readers sit up; vague ones get ignored.
3. **Can nobody else say it?** Could a competitor sign this line unchanged? If yes, rewrite it.

Three no's: probably filler. Three yes's: you're onto something.

- Fails all three: "Don't just get a job, change an entire industry." You can't picture it, can't check it, and any recruiter could say it.
- Passes all three: New Balance, "worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." You can picture both, both are true, and no other brand owns that pairing.

## 1. Visualize: abstract → concrete

- Concrete nouns stick (pitbull, leg of lamb). Abstract ones fade (seamless transition, better way). Test: can you drop it on your foot?
- **Zoom ladder:** write the abstract word at the top of a page and keep asking "what do I actually mean?" until you reach an object or a scene.
  - "Regain fitness" → "get off the couch after six months" → "run 5K" → *Couch to 5K*.
- Go one level more specific than feels natural: "pretty people in big cities" → "supermodels in London."
- **Metonymy:** swap a literal word for a visual one ("1,000 songs in your *pocket*," not "in your media player").
- Pair a line with an image that shows the same thing (Volvo: "your speedometer has five numbers, ours has six").

## 2. Falsify: point, don't talk

- **"You can't talk, you can only point."** Replace adjectives with evidence the reader can check: a chart, a number, a named person, a behavior.
  - Dating-profile drill: "funny, smart, good values" → "6'2", looks like Ryan Gosling, reads on the tube."
  - Selling gold: don't call it a great investment. Point at a 50-year price chart, or at how it behaved in the last crash.
- **Facts first.** If in doubt, start with a fact. There's usually a story behind it, and a fact guarantees you're saying *something*.
  - Facts don't have to be numbers ("Even when it's not Heinz, it's Heinz" came from watching cheap ketchup being poured into Heinz bottles).
  - Not every fact is interesting. Work it until it means something to the reader.
  - Commentary test: "they're playing badly" is an opinion. "A right-back is playing at left-back" is a fact.
- Precise numbers feel truer ("3.1 seconds").
- Modest, believable claims beat big ones: "lift conversions from 1% to 2%" (that's twice as many) beats "become a millionaire."
- Research is the cost of falsifiable copy. That's why it beats cheap adjectives.

## 3. Only you can say it

- "Never write an ad a competitor can sign."
- Ask: **what can we do that nobody else is doing, that people care about?** (A journeyman footballer outsold star memoirs by writing an anonymous insider tell-all as *The Secret Footballer*.)
- Find the claim only your product can make: "They don't write songs about Volvos" for a car people do write songs about. "Tougher than an F-150, faster than a 911" for a truck no other vehicle can claim both of.
- **Explain a new category by comparing it to things people already know.**
- **Positioning:** what do you replace? ("You're going to need a smaller cabinet" for an all-in-one supplement.) Leave a crowded bucket by naming the real problem (Loom moved from "screen recorder" to "async video, because remote meetings suck").

## Devices that make lines stick

- **Contrast / juxtaposition:** two things that shouldn't fit ("make two months' salary last forever," "the dating app designed to be deleted").
- **Enemy:** there are three kinds of opponent: a different approach, a different belief, a competitor. Have a cheap shot at the alternative.
- **Parallelism and rhythm:** matched structures ("22,000 hours writing… spend 2 learning"). Tweak numbers or words to create symmetry, but keep them truthful.
- **Alliteration** when it fits naturally ("dating app designed to be deleted").
- **Make the customer feel smart** about choosing you ("for people who inherited brains instead of wealth").
- **Reframe the cost:** widen the time frame so the ask looks tiny (4 hours a day becomes 22,000 hours in a career, against 2 hours to learn).
- **Break the fourth wall:** "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad, the time a Model S takes to go 0–60."
- **Fewer elements:** a typical ad has headline, subhead, picture and logo. If you can make it work with one of them, you're onto a winner. An ad that doesn't look like an ad gets read.

## The 2-second test

Show the piece and count "one Mississippi, two Mississippi." The reader should get it before you finish. An ad that's confusing but gets talked about beats a cliché nobody notices; not everyone has to get it.
