# Lists, Testing, Arithmetic & Focus

## Lists

- Better list = biggest response lever. Wrong list (nurses' offer mailed to secretaries) fails whatever the copy.
- Ranking: past buyers of similar products (RFM: recency, frequency, monetary) > trade-magazine subscribers > licensed/registered universe > broad compiled lists.
- Get the best list broker you can; they find lists you don't know exist (course enrollees, event attendees, buyers of adjacent products).
- Overlays/modeling: profile your buyers (income, education, urban/rural, car value, demographics) and select lookalikes; drop low-performing segments. Some segments respond 30× better than others.
- Test a universe with an **nth-name** sample (every 10th/20th name, ~5,000 pieces) before rolling out.
- Use merge/purge to remove existing customers and duplicates; verify list freshness (bounces/returns signal quality).
- Don't exclude markets out of pride (e.g. the "opportunity seeker" market) if your product genuinely serves them.

## Testing mindset

- Marketing is a **process, not an event**. Keep what works, drop what doesn't — like a bar singer cycling songs until the set is all hits (ratchet effect).
- Test everything: list, offer, price, guarantee, headline, story, format (letter vs. tear sheet vs. postcard), grabber, delivery speed, personalization, ad size, response channel.
- Once you have a winner, the common failure is *not making enough mistakes* — throw many challengers at the wall.
- You'll tire of your copy long before the audience does; replace on results, not boredom. But all copy eventually fatigues (ads ~6–12 months) — always have the next control in development.
- Knock off your own winner before competitors do.
- When results don't compute, distrust the data first: verify pieces were actually sent and counted (people have found whole mailings sitting in warehouses).
- Don't trust secondhand numbers ("adding a phone number dropped response") — test in your own market.
- Small complaints among many sales aren't a reason to change a working piece.

## Arithmetic

Everything is arithmetic. Work it out explicitly for the user:

- Cost per piece × pieces → cost of send. Response rate → leads. Conversion → sales. Sales × margin (after fulfillment) − send cost = profit per send.
- Example: 1,000 pieces at $0.50 = $500 → 5% call = 50 leads → +$500 follow-up → 10% close = 5 sales × $600 = $3,000 → ~$2,000 profit per 1,000. Multiply by universe size to size the opportunity.
- Upgrade math: priority package adds ~$6/lead; on 100 leads that's $600 — you need ~1 extra $600 sale to break even. Ask "how many more sales justify this?" — usually trivially few.
- Judge each list by profit per name; keep mailing down the list (gold → silver → copper coins) until it stops paying, not until it's worse than your best.
- Don't require new channels to beat the current one — if profitable at all, run it too.
- **Snowball rollout**: mail a tested winner in waves, converting each wave's orders into the next wave's postage — a big universe without big capital.
- Ignore hourly-rate thinking; think lifestyle and leverage.

## Investing in copy

- Hire the best copywriter you can afford — amortized over every piece sent, it's the cheapest part of the campaign.
- The founder's main job: do the marketing or see that it gets done and improved continuously. Everything else (accounting, ops, fulfillment) is easier to hire.
- Business is a circle; shade only the slice only you can do.
- Spend time in "important, not urgent" work (Covey Q2); writing and testing the main promotion is that work.
- Pick up gold coins first: exploit what's already working before chasing new ideas. Idea-chasing is a common way to sabotage profits.
- Understanding is the booby prize — implementation wins. Do it, even imperfectly.

## Creativity techniques

- Start from the absurd: "What offer would make literally everyone respond?" then back off to something affordable (e.g. $100 to visit → settled at $20, ~20% came in).
- Write the ad you *wish* were true, as strongly as possible, then edit to what's true.
- Brainstorm when fatigued/loose — inhibitions drop and wild ideas surface; nothing said in brainstorming has to be printed. Keep critics out of the room.
- Write with peers and trade drafts — others see angles you don't.
- Keep a swipe file and headline cards; adapt proven structures.
- Recommended reading: Schwab *How to Write a Good Advertisement*, Hopkins *Scientific Advertising*, Caples *Tested Advertising Methods*, Collier *Letter Book*, Ogilvy, Schwartz *Breakthrough Advertising* (advanced) — and sales books (salesmanship is the foundation).
