# Writing Process & Simplicity (Harry Dry)

How a piece goes from a seed to a finished line, and how to write simply.

## Frame the job: A → B

- The consumer's current attitude is the start line and the desired attitude is the finish line (John Hegarty). Name both before writing; the copy is the wire strung between two poles.
- Copywriting has three pieces:
  1. **Who you're talking to.** Say where they are and what they're doing. A tourist strolling past a café needs "coffee + pastry = £5," not a 17-item menu. Mismatched audience burns budget (a youth app advertised to an audience averaging 39 years old). Form follows function.
  2. **Something to say.** A belief, or something you think is wrong with the world. Counter-position against what annoys you. A "why" makes copy easier and more believable.
  3. **Say it well.** Clear, visual, memorable. Most people think copywriting is only this step; it's the last third.

## See it where the reader will

- Write in the final medium: newsletters in the email tool, ads in the design tool, landing pages on the page. Line breaks and length change everything.
- Mock a billboard or ad *among competitors and real surroundings*, not alone on a white canvas. Ask whether it still grabs attention.
- Copy and design are one job. Pick the layout, hierarchy and images alongside the words.

## Iterate from a seed

Worked example: one ad took about 25 rewrites over 2 days.
1. **Seed:** a striking fact or observation ("going from 1% to 2% conversion is a 100% increase").
2. **Borrow a structure** from a great ad you've saved (e.g. a two-column "ordinary way vs. our way" checklist layout). Collect excellent work so you have references.
3. **Conflict column:** draw a line down the page and write opposites that come to mind ("throw money at ads" vs. "learn copywriting"). Don't overthink it.
4. **Spitball without judging** (the "dirty tap"): run the tap until the water runs clean. Write bad options fast; the good ones arrive after.
5. **Fix the form:** make key lines fit on one line, give parallel headings symmetry, and keep the rhythm (twice / twice / twice / both). Solving one problem often creates another; keep going.
6. **Get feedback on versions, not a single draft.** Show 3 options and ask which line wins. People can choose far better than they can critique.
7. **Finish with light design emphasis** (bold the key phrase) and art direction that matches the story.

Keep going after "this is good." It often gets twice as good. Your work is your standards; only ship what's 9/10 or better.

## Write simply = rewrite

- You can't write simply on the first pass, only rewrite simply. Draft several versions of the same paragraph side by side and merge the best parts.
- **Kaplan's Law of Words:** any word that isn't working for you is working against you. It applies to ideas too: an idea's strength is inversely proportional to its scope.
  - "And" on a landing page is usually a red flag. Do one thing ("We make jeans. That's it.").
- **Paragraph test:** pull out a sentence. If the paragraph still works, the sentence shouldn't have been there. A good paragraph holds together like a burrito you can throw.
- Short paragraphs (~2 lines) work like monkey bars: easy to swing between on a phone. A 3-line paragraph often means you haven't explained it clearly yet.
- Give strong lines room to breathe.
- **Done test:** walk away, come back, and try to remove anything. If nothing can be added or removed, it's done.

## Structure

- Structure = **dividing lines + parallelism**. Split a big topic into 2–3 named buckets ("who / what / how"; "first 100 readers / next 9,900").
- Interesting framings ("That's Interesting," 1971): show that two things people think are different are really one thing, or that one thing is really two.

## Story & conflict

- Conflict lives in the "but": find what went wrong ("I woke at 8:52… but Mom said I couldn't wear those shorts…").
- People remember relatively (pickle juice makes orange juice taste sweeter). Show the before, then the after; how everyone else does it, then how this one does it.
- **Letters (email/newsletter):** it's less about the news and more about the letter. Open with time, place and what's going on right now, taken from what's actually in front of you ("3:47 a.m., green tea gone cold, the intro left to write"). Pack it densely with examples, with conflict in each.

## Taste, conviction, experience

- AI is a tool and is only as good as your taste. It predicts what has been written before; good writing arranges ideas in a way they haven't been arranged before.
- **Experience the product yourself.** Ogilvy's famous Rolls-Royce line about the electric clock being the loudest noise came from a real driving report. Great books come from years lived inside the subject. Gather firsthand observations before writing; don't stitch together clichés ("yellow taxis, the city that never sleeps").
- Conviction: the writer has to believe something.
- Protect deep work. Silence before input: no feeds first thing, a walk without headphones, focused sessions.
